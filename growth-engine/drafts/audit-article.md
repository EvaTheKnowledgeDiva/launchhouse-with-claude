## 1. Blog post

- **Title:** Respect Creates Respect: What a Clock-In Meeting Taught Me About Audits
- **Short description (for search and link previews):** I left my para job to make life easier for sped teachers. Nashville wants an audit of its schools. Here is why I say bring it on, and why audits should not be scary.
- **Web address ending:** respect-creates-respect-audits

**Body:**

In May of 2026, I left my job as a paraprofessional because I wanted to spend my time making life a bit easier for special education (sped) teachers. I worked with some amazingly tough, deeply caring people, but I noticed that the higher-ups didn't care for us the same way.

We had gotten a new system to clock in and had a meeting to walk us through the new operation and expectations. Now mind you, the principal was ex-military, so I get the tone. My dad was ex-Navy and would do the same to us. You can take the person out of the military, but you can't take the military out of them.

"You WILL get caught if you clock in at the wrong location. We HAVE CAMERAS at every clock-in location. If you lie, YOU WILL BE CAUGHT." I was shocked. I wanted to say, "I am sorry, but we are not juvenile delinquents here. We are professional adults who know the expectations. No need to threaten us." But alas, I kept my CEO/management-trained mouth shut, because this was NOT my circus, and these weren't my monkeys, so to speak.

Now, in October 2026, the school system that I used to work for is having an AUDIT demanded. Audits are routine inspections, if you will, to help guide schools to best practices and standards. Nothing to fear if you are doing education right. But I'm not so sure things were being done right either, so I say, "Bring it on!"

Rewind to 2017 and 2018. I had gotten an entry-level call center job helping seniors choose their Medicare plans. First we sold the plans, then we helped people use their plans to the best of our and their ability. I had done a summer as a pharmacy tech, so I was handy at asking questions to help seniors choose the best plan based on what they take.

"Ma'am, what color is the pill?" … "And it's used for what ailment?" … "Bear with me… is it called cyclobenzaprine or Flexeril?" … "Excellent! What's your next medication?" This process takes time, care, knowledge, patience, and more time. Some "agents," as we were called, were all about the money. Get a high turnover of calls so you can sell the most and make a great commission. I was raised on "the love of money is the root of all evil," so it's rarely a motivator for me. Evil Eva is not a nickname that I aspire to, but boy, does it roll off the tongue.

As a kid, my best friend was old enough to be my great-grandmother, so I saw these callers as potential friends, not old, annoying people with bad health. I was also raised to respect elders, learn from them, and care for them, because at one point in time, they cared for me too. Now it was my turn.

I took that same worldview into being a paraprofessional. The principal had no idea that I used to train about 500 adults in Medicare compliance, half locally, half virtually. 500 is a lot of "students" to care for, and I was training them to care for others. And it was stressful, because if they made a mistake, I had to fix it.

Did I ever yell at my coworkers to motivate them? Nope, because it doesn't work. It doesn't get them to learn. If anything, it teaches them not to ask questions, to keep their mouths shut, and to avoid speaking with me. Not a good management strategy at all. From my own experience, respect creates respect. I respect my students. They are not beneath me. I am just their guide. I help them along their way. If they take the help, great! If they don't, that is on them.

Audits are beneficial. They are the check to make sure things are being done the effective, right way. Taxes get audited, and so do businesses, schools, hospitals, governments, etc. If you are an organization, someone is going to check in occasionally and make sure things are being done the right way.

I live in Nashville, and they are calling for an extra audit of the schools. I agree. It needs to be done. It's another set of eyes. It shouldn't be feared, but it is stressful for all involved. And I am spending my time building a software service that paras, teachers, BCBAs, and therapists can use to help keep up with the data that is scrutinized in audits. Why? Because I care. And I know that there is a better way to motivate than screaming at people.

*This post first appeared on my Substack.*

### Post 1: the clock-in meeting

We had a staff meeting about the new clock-in system, and the message was: "You WILL get caught. We HAVE CAMERAS."

I sat there thinking, we are professional adults. We know the expectations. No need to threaten us.

Sped teachers and paras carry some of the heaviest days in the building, and so often we get talked to like suspects instead of professionals. Respect creates respect. Yelling just teaches people to stop asking questions.

Sped teachers and paras, have you sat in a meeting like that? Tell me below.

### Post 2: bring on the audit

Nashville is calling for an extra audit of its schools, and I say bring it on.

Audits are routine. Taxes get audited, and so do hospitals, businesses and governments. It's another set of eyes, and if the work is being done right, there's nothing to fear.

But I know why it still feels scary for the teachers and paras in the room. The work got done. The proof is on a paper tracker, a sticky note, or in somebody's memory. Most of the time the problem isn't the work. It's the paperwork that has to prove it.

### Post 3: 500 adults and no yelling

Before I was a para, I trained about 500 adults in Medicare compliance, half in person and half virtually. Every mistake they made, I had to fix.

Did I ever yell at them to motivate them? Nope. Yelling doesn't teach. It teaches people to stop asking questions, keep their mouths shut and avoid you.

I took that same thinking into special education. My students were never beneath me. I was their guide. Respect creates respect.
